# Adjacent fields: what they say about the field, its dimension, and two clocks

A lateral search for `theory.md` (§§0, B, C, D, F) and `frontier.md`. It asks how
general equilibrium economics, consumer–resource and niche theory, data
assimilation, linear response theory, reinforcement learning and control,
heterogeneous-agent macroeconomics and mean-field games, and ecosystem
demography models represent the structure TF24 has:
- a population makes a field;
- individuals respond to the field;
- the resident sits at a demographic equilibrium;
- invasion fitness is a functional of the field;
- adjoints give reproductive value and field sensitivities.

Each section gives the correspondence, what it implies for state variables and
dimension, the metric question, the algorithms that transfer, and where the
correspondence fails. §8 gives recommendations for the domain representation.

Labels:
- **[Verified]**: the claim was checked against an abstract or full text retrieved
  in this search (PubMed, the publisher, the paper's PDF, or a repository).
- **[Ref]**: the bibliographic record (authors, year, venue, DOI) was verified
  against Crossref. The content statement is standard textbook knowledge or my
  recollection of the paper, not re-read here.
- **[Derived]**: my own derivation for this model, unchecked by anyone else.
- **[Judgement]**: an assessment, open to dispute.

Notation follows `theory.md`: E is the field, S(θ) = δφ(θ; E)/δE the invader's
field sensitivity, I the impact (how a strategy's presence moves E*), g the
held-field selection gradient, F_θ the field-responding derivative,
Φ = F_θ − g the field term.

## 1. General equilibrium economics

### 1.1 The correspondence

**Which object is the price.** The obvious mapping is "field = price vector".
That mapping is wrong in one respect that matters [Judgement]. In a
competitive economy every agent faces the same price vector p, and at an
interior optimum each agent's marginal rates of substitution equal p. In
TF24 the field E is a vector of *quantities* (light flux at each height,
water content in each layer), not of prices. The objects that behave like
prices are the duals: S(θ) is the marginal fitness value of each field
element *to strategy θ*. These marginal values differ across strategies and
nothing equalises them, because there is no exchange. In the Meszéna
framework this is the distinction between the regulating variable (a
quantity) and the sensitivity (a covector over it). So:

| general equilibrium | TF24 |
|---|---|
| commodity space | field space: (z, ℓ) × patch age a (× calendar time t, × weather state ω, in a two-clock model) |
| quantity vector (endowment, net supply) | the field E |
| price vector p | S(θ): a strategy-specific shadow price over E; no common price |
| firm with constant returns, profit p·y(θ) | a strategy; invasion fitness φ(θ; E) |
| free entry: active firms earn zero profit, inactive ones ≤ 0 | residents have φ = 0; non-invadable means φ(θ′; E*) ≤ 0 for all θ′ |
| market clearing | the field E* is generated by the residents at b* |
| externality (no market for an effect on others) | each resident's effect on the field it shares (§F.3) |

The free-entry row is the closest match. A competitive equilibrium with
constant-returns activities is a set of activity levels and prices such that
active activities break even and no inactive activity is profitable. An ESS
community is a set of residents at demographic equilibrium such that each has
zero invasion fitness and no mutant has positive invasion fitness. [Judgement]

**Linear programming and competitive exclusion.** In a linear program with m
constraints, a basic optimal solution has at most m nonzero activities, and
complementary slackness says an activity is used only if its reduced cost
(cost minus the dual prices of the resources it uses) is zero [Ref: standard
LP duality; Dantzig 1963 is the usual citation, not checked here]. Read with
species as activities and limiting resources as constraints, this is the
competitive exclusion principle (at most m species on m resources at a
stable equilibrium; Levin 1970), and the reduced-cost condition is the zero
net growth isocline (ZNGI) condition. MacArthur (1970) showed that the
competitive equilibrium of his consumer–resource model minimises a quadratic
form [Ref]; Gatto (1990) generalised such minimum principles [Ref, title only].
The analogy is exact only when per-capita growth is linear in the regulating
variables, the assumption Levin's proof needs (§2.1).

**Dated and contingent commodities.** In Arrow–Debreu (1954; Debreu 1959) a
commodity is a physical good indexed by date, location and state of nature.
With L goods, T dates and S states there are L·T·S commodities, and a
complete market needs a price for each. Arrow (1964) showed that with S
state-contingent securities and spot markets in each state, the same
allocations are reachable with far fewer markets; Radner (1972) defined the
equilibrium of such a sequence of markets [Ref, all three]. Barabás, Meszéna
& Ostling (2012) state the ecological counterpart: in a periodic environment
"all factors that function to regulate population growth may be considered
as separate regulating factors for each moment of time", so "a single
resource becomes a resource continuum" [Verified, abstract via repository
excerpt].

**Comparative statics.** Samuelson's (1947) comparative statics apply the
implicit function theorem to equilibrium conditions. His correspondence
principle uses the stability of the equilibrium to sign comparative-static
derivatives [Ref: book, standard]. Debreu (1970) showed that equilibria are
generically locally unique ("regular"), so the implicit function theorem
applies almost everywhere, with folds on a measure-zero set of economies
[Ref].

`theory.md`'s (I4), d ln b*/dθ = −F_θ/F_{ln b}, is the one-dimensional case.
F_{ln b} < 0 is the stability condition of the demographic equilibrium, so by
the correspondence principle sign(d ln b*/dθ) = sign(F_θ) wherever the
equilibrium is stable [Derived]. Folds (F_{ln b} → 0) are Debreu's critical
economies. The constant-rain jump (`frontier.md` §1.3) is not a fold. It is a
non-smooth equilibrium map, and the general equilibrium analogue is an
economy with kinked (Leontief or linear) technologies. There, comparative
statics are one-sided and the implicit function theorem does not apply
[Judgement].

**Externalities, the welfare theorem, Pigouvian prices.** The first welfare
theorem needs complete markets and no externalities. Shared light and water
are unpriced effects of each individual on others, so the theorem does not
apply. This is Hardin's (1968) commons [Ref]. A Pigouvian tax sets the
private price of an action equal to its marginal external cost at the
optimum.

In TF24, `theory.md` (F.2) gives λ^res − λ^inv as the marginal externality of
a state, and §F.4 gives λ_W^stand as the stand's price of water. The
Pigouvian price on transpiration that would make the selfish leaf choose the
stand optimum is the externality per unit uptake, evaluated at the stand
optimum, not at the ESS [Derived]. The same distinction appears in
mean-field games (§6.1) as the difference between a Nash equilibrium and a
McKean–Vlasov optimal control.

**Sonnenschein–Mantel–Debreu.** Sonnenschein (1972), Mantel (1974) and Debreu
(1974) showed that aggregate excess demand is restricted only by continuity,
Walras' law and homogeneity, given enough agents relative to goods [Ref, all
three; the summary is confirmed by a secondary source]. Individual
rationality therefore imposes almost no structure on aggregate responses.
Brown & Matzkin (1996) recovered testable restrictions by looking at the
*equilibrium manifold*: the joint relation between endowments, incomes and
equilibrium prices [Verified, abstract]. Hildenbrand (1983) obtained
aggregate structure (a law of demand) from assumptions on the *distribution*
of agents, not on each agent [Ref].

### 1.2 What it implies for state, sufficient statistics and dimension

- **A low-dimensional field cannot be derived from equilibrium logic.**
  SMD's lesson for H1 is that a community's aggregate response map
  θ → E*(θ) has no low-dimensional structure merely because each individual
  responds to E through a smooth G. If low dimension exists, it comes from
  the particular physics: allometry, Beer's-law light, buckets of soil water
  [Judgement]. H1 is therefore an empirical claim about TF24, not a theorem
  awaiting proof.
- **Test the joint relation.** Following Brown–Matzkin, the more informative
  object is the joint map (θ, c) → (E*, S, b*), not E* alone. For H1 this
  means testing selection modes against both traits and climate, not only
  the field response [Judgement].
- **Commodity count is not dimension.** Arrow–Debreu and Barabás et al.
  (2012) agree that resolving time and states multiplies the coordinates.
  Whether the extra coordinates matter is a rank question (§7) [Judgement].
  So H2 in its weak form, "more coordinates", is true by construction. Its
  substantive form concerns effective rank.

### 1.3 Metric and basis

In general equilibrium the pairing p·x between prices and quantities is
invariant under any change of commodity units: prices transform inversely to
quantities. The same holds for ⟨S, δE⟩ in TF24. Statements built only from
such pairings (profit, invasion fitness, g = −⟨S, dE*/dθ⟩) are basis-free.
Statements about "how many directions of E matter", taken from S alone, are
not, because they need a norm on field space. Economics avoids the issue by
counting goods, not directions. §7 takes this up.

### 1.4 Transferable

- The free-entry and LP-duality reading of coexistence. It gives a
  computational check: a candidate community is an ESS community exactly
  when the maximum over θ′ of φ(θ′; E*) is 0 and is attained at the
  residents. This is an LP-like complementarity condition, and could be
  posed as one if φ were linearised in E [Judgement].
- The correspondence principle, as a sign check on the (I4) predictor.
- Pigouvian accounting: report λ^res − λ^inv per state, per age and per
  channel as "the tax that would align the ESS with the stand optimum".

### 1.5 Where it breaks

- **No exchange.** Marginal values are not equalised across strategies, so
  there is no single price system and no efficiency benchmark. The "stand
  optimum" (max b*) is one possible group objective among several (max
  biomass, max NPP), with no normative standing [Judgement].
- **Selection does not optimise.** Agents in general equilibrium optimise
  continuously; strategies in TF24 are fixed. Selection reaches the
  free-entry condition only at an ESS, and only through mutation-limited
  substitution.
- **Entry is local.** Free entry in economics is global (any technology may
  enter). Invasion analysis by gradients is local; the global ESS condition
  needs a search over θ′.

## 2. Consumer–resource and niche theory

### 2.1 Established results

- **Tilman (1980, 1982).** Each species has a ZNGI in resource space (where
  its growth is zero) and a consumption (impact) vector; a supply point is
  given. Two species coexist stably where their ZNGIs cross, if each consumes
  relatively more of the resource that limits it [Ref].
- **MacArthur (1970); Chesson (1990).** Chesson showed how MacArthur's
  consumer–resource model reduces to Lotka–Volterra competition, with
  competition coefficients built from overlap in resource use [Ref].
- **Levin (1970).** "No stable equilibrium can be attained in an ecological
  community in which some r of the components are limited by less than r
  limiting factors" [Verified, abstract via bibliography excerpt].
- **Linearity matters.** Armstrong & McGehee (1980), building on their 1976
  paper, showed that the proof needs per-capita growth to be linear in the
  factors. Without linearity, more species than factors can coexist, but not
  at fixed densities [Verified via a secondary description of the 1976
  abstract; 1980 bibliographic record verified].
- **Non-equilibrium routes.** Huisman & Weissing (1999) showed that
  oscillations and chaos in plankton resource competition let more species
  coexist than there are resources [Ref]. Hutchinson's (1961) paradox of the
  plankton is the original observation [Ref].
- **Regulating factors.** Meszéna et al. (2006) define sensitivity and impact
  vectors over regulating factors [Ref, in `theory.md`].
- **Structured populations.** Szilágyi & Meszéna (2009) extend this to
  structured populations [Verified, abstract]:
  - "the niche of a species is specified by the impact and sensitivity niche
    vectors";
  - "robust coexistence requires sufficient segregation of the impact, as
    well as of the sensitivity niche vectors";
  - "each i-state contributes to the impact of the population proportional
    to its frequency";
  - "sensitivity of the population is composed of the sensitivity of the
    rates of demographic transitions, weighted by the frequency and by the
    reproductive value of the initial and final i-states of the transition".
- **No infinite coexistence.** Gyllenberg & Meszéna (2005): the coexistence
  of infinitely many strategies is structurally unstable [Ref].
- **Envelopes.** Koffel, Daufresne, Massol & Klausmeier (2016) extend
  Tilman's graphics to a continuum of strategies using the envelope of
  ZNGIs. This gives a "close correspondence between the classification of
  singular strategies and the geometrical properties of the envelope"
  [Verified, abstract].
- **Facilitation and mutualism.** Koffel, Daufresne & Klausmeier (2021)
  extend this to a general theory of the niche [Ref].
- **Light in a well-mixed column.** In Huisman & Weissing's (1994) model, the
  species with the lowest critical light intensity (light at the bottom of
  the column in monoculture) excludes the others [Verified via secondary
  descriptions]. Huisman, van Oostveen & Weissing (1999) showed that at low
  mixing rates, position in the light gradient becomes a further niche axis
  [Verified via secondary description].
- **Light in forests.** Adams, Purves & Pacala (2007) give "a simple whole
  life-cycle measure of performance" for trees competing for light. When
  species differ in everything except crown transmissivity, competition is
  hierarchical and "the equivalent of R* is Z*, the height at which trees
  enter the canopy in an equilibrium monoculture". In the general case,
  coexistence, founder control or exclusion are all possible [Verified,
  abstract].
- **The PPA.** Strigul et al. (2008)'s perfect plasticity approximation
  represents the forest by von Foerster PDEs "coupled with an integral
  equation", the equation for the canopy closure height [Verified, abstract].
- **Size asymmetry.** Schwinning & Weiner (1998) analyse the mechanisms that
  set the degree of size asymmetry in plant competition. Light competition is
  the standard example of strong asymmetry, because larger plants shade
  smaller ones but not the reverse [Ref; content via secondary sources].
- **Empirical sensitivity and impact.** Kunstler et al. (2016), with data
  from 3 million trees, report that "low wood density was also correlated
  with a low ability to tolerate competition and a low competitive effect on
  neighbours, while high specific leaf area was correlated with a low
  competitive effect" [Verified, abstract].

### 2.2 The correspondence

**Szilágyi & Meszéna (2009) is the exact antecedent of `frontier.md`'s (S, I)
pair.** Their population sensitivity, "demographic transition sensitivities
weighted by frequency and reproductive value", is `theory.md` (B.3) and
(F.1) with finitely many i-states. Their impact, "frequency-weighted",
is TF24's I. `theory.md` currently paraphrases Meszéna et al. (2006) from
memory. Citing Szilágyi & Meszéna (2009) would anchor B.3 and F.2 in a
published result for structured populations [Judgement].

**ZNGI normals are sensitivities.** The ZNGI of θ is the level set
φ(θ; E) = 0 in field space. Its normal is S(θ). Tilman's consumption vector
is I(θ). Coexistence of i and j in Tilman's sense is first-order mutual
invasibility, ⟨S_i, E_j − E_i⟩ > 0 and ⟨S_j, E_i − E_j⟩ > 0 (`theory.md`
B.3). The stability condition corresponds to Meszéna's requirement that the
matrix A_ij = ⟨S_i, I_j⟩ be far from singular [Derived].

**Light by height becomes a resource only under conditions.**
- In a well-mixed column, the light gradient collapses to one scalar, the
  critical light intensity: one regulating factor.
- With size-structured plants that have perfectly plastic crowns and differ
  in everything except transmissivity, it collapses to one scalar per patch
  age, the canopy closure height z*(a) (Adams et al. 2007; Strigul et al.
  2008).
- TF24 has a continuous light profile and no perfect plasticity, so the
  collapse is approximate at best. But it predicts where light's effective
  dimension comes from: not from height, which collapses towards one number
  per patch, but from patch age, through the trajectory z*(a) over
  succession [Judgement].
- So for H1, the leading light modes should look like functionals of the
  canopy-closure trajectory: early versus late succession. They should not
  look like independent heights [Judgement; testable].

**Water by time becomes a resource continuum** (Barabás et al. 2012). But
soil storage smooths the record before any plant sees it. A plant's
sensitivity to rain on day d is a convolution of its sensitivity to soil
water with the bucket's impulse response. Two strategies can have
non-collinear sensitivities to rain on two days only if they differ in how
they read the soil over the bucket's memory time, through rooting depth,
plant water storage or timing of demand [Derived].

This gives a concrete prediction for H2: the effective rank of
water-by-time sensitivities across strategies is bounded by roughly the
record length divided by the shortest soil or plant memory time that
strategies can differentially exploit, times the number of independently
read layers. Variability adds effective dimensions only on time scales
longer than that memory [Judgement; testable with the existing per-layer
field].

**Size asymmetry and the constant-rain jump.** Complete size asymmetry makes
an individual's light depend on rank order of height, and rank order is
discontinuous in traits at ties. Kisdi (1999), cited in `theory.md`, and the
measured ln R′ jump of about 8 within ±0.01% of `lma` are consistent with
this. Schwinning & Weiner's framework says the degree of asymmetry is a
mechanism property that can be tuned. A model with partial asymmetry (crown
overlap, light scattering) should restore differentiability, at a cost in
realism [Judgement].

### 2.3 Dimension

- **Levin counts factors at a stable equilibrium with linear dependence.**
  With an infinite-dimensional field (Barabás et al. 2012), the count that
  matters is the number of species whose sensitivity and impact vectors can
  be kept well separated. Gyllenberg & Meszéna (2005) show this number is
  finite generically. Limiting-similarity theory says it is set by how
  smooth the sensitivity functions are relative to their spread [Judgement,
  from the cited framework].
- **A second route to more species than factors** is non-equilibrium
  dynamics: Armstrong & McGehee (1980); Huisman & Weissing (1999). The
  current TF24 cannot represent it, because its regional state is
  stationary. A two-clock model with a fluctuating b(t) can (§8). So H2 has
  two parts, and a rank computation at one field tests only the first:
  - more regulating factors, testable by the rank of S;
  - non-equilibrium coexistence through relative nonlinearity, testable
    only by second-order or full runs.

### 2.4 Metric and basis

Meszéna's robustness criterion uses the species × species matrix
A_ij = ⟨S_i, I_j⟩. Under any linear change of field coordinates, S transforms
covariantly and I contravariantly, so A is unchanged [Derived, elementary].
Its singular values depend only on how species (or traits) are scaled. That
makes A a basis-free carrier of "how many independent ways the community is
regulated". The spectrum of S alone, or of I alone, is not basis-free.

### 2.5 Transferable

- The (S, I) definitions for structured populations from Szilágyi & Meszéna
  (2009), including the reproductive-value weighting.
- Koffel et al.'s envelope construction, projected onto the two or three
  leading field modes once they are found. It gives a picture of the ESS
  community and its bifurcations along a climate gradient.
- Z* (Adams et al. 2007) as a candidate one-number summary of the light
  field per patch age, to test against the leading light mode.
- Kunstler et al. (2016) as an empirical benchmark. TF24's S (tolerance)
  and I (effect) should reproduce the signs they report for wood density and
  SLA, if TF24 parameters map onto those traits [Judgement].

### 2.6 Where it breaks

- Tilman's theory assumes consumable, well-mixed resource pools at
  equilibrium. Light is pre-empted, not consumed from a pool, and its
  "concentration" is position-dependent.
- Water in TF24 is depleted per patch and per layer, which fits the theory
  better.
- Neither resource has a single supply point. The patch-age structure makes
  every patch age its own local "chemostat", with p(a) as weights.

## 3. Data assimilation and climate science

### 3.1 Established methods

- **Adjoints in data assimilation.** Le Dimet & Talagrand (1986) laid the
  variational and adjoint foundations of 4D-Var [Ref]. Errico (1997) is the
  standard explanation of adjoint models [Ref]. Giering & Kaminski (1998)
  give recipes for adjoint code construction, the source-transformation
  approach used for MITgcm-type models [Ref].
- **Adjoints of large models.** Marotzke et al. (1999) built the adjoint of
  the MIT ocean model and computed the sensitivity of Atlantic heat
  transport to forcing and state [Ref].
- **Adjoint attribution of an event.** Kauker et al. (2009) used the adjoint
  of a coupled ocean–sea-ice model to attribute the September 2007 ice
  minimum. They traced 86% of the area reduction to four factors: May and
  June winds, September 2 m temperature and March ice thickness. Two-thirds
  was set by factors known by the end of June [Verified, abstract via
  repository].
- **Ice-sheet sensitivity.** Heimbach & Bugnion (2009) gave adjoint
  sensitivities of Greenland ice volume to basal, surface and initial
  conditions [Ref].
- **Adjoints in land-surface models.** Kaminski et al. (2013) describe the
  BETHY/JSBACH carbon-cycle data-assimilation system, an adjoint-based
  terrestrial vegetation system [Ref].
- **Singular vectors.** Lorenz (1965) introduced the leading singular
  vectors of the tangent propagator in a 28-variable model [Ref]. Buizza &
  Palmer (1995) mapped them for the global circulation [Ref]. Farrell &
  Ioannou (1996, Parts I and II) generalised stability theory to
  non-normal, autonomous and non-autonomous operators [Ref].
- **The metric of singular vectors.** Palmer, Gelaro, Barkmeijer & Buizza
  (1998) [Verified, abstract]:
  - "there is, in principle, no freedom in the choice of inner product or
    metric for the singular vector calculation. However, the correct metric
    is dependent on the purpose";
  - for predictability studies, a first-order approximation to the
    appropriate metric is perturbation energy.

  Barkmeijer, van Gijzen & Bouttier (1998) use the analysis-error covariance
  (the inverse Hessian of the 4D-Var cost) as the initial-time metric [Ref].
- **Ensemble sensitivity.** Torn & Hakim (2008) introduced ensemble
  sensitivity: the regression of a forecast metric on initial state across
  an ensemble [Ref]. Ancell & Hakim (2007) compared it with adjoint
  sensitivity [Ref]. Ensemble sensitivity equals the adjoint sensitivity
  premultiplied by the ensemble covariance and divided by each variable's
  variance [Ref; relation stated in a secondary description of Ancell's
  work].
- **Large dimension.** Kalmikov & Heimbach (2014) approximate the Hessian of
  a global ocean state estimate with low-rank Lanczos methods [Ref]. Halko,
  Martinsson & Tropp (2011) give randomised low-rank decompositions that
  need only matrix–vector products [Ref].
- **Gradient-based dimension reduction.** Constantine, Dow & Wang (2014)
  find "active subspaces" from the eigenvectors of E[∇f ∇fᵀ] [Ref]. Zahm et
  al. (2020) extend this to vector-valued outputs [Ref].

### 3.2 The correspondence

**4D-Var's gradient with respect to forcing** is the two-stage adjoint of
`theory.md` B.6, which gives dφ_i/dc(day). The Kauker et al. attribution is
the same calculation applied to a realised anomaly:
δφ ≈ Σ_d (∂φ/∂c_d) δc_d, decomposed by day and channel. TF24 can do this for
"which days of a drought cost the invader fitness" with machinery that is
specified but not built [Derived]. Like Kauker et al., it is first order and
must be checked against a run with the anomaly removed. TF24 can do that
check cheaply, because an invader run in a counterfactual field costs one
run.

**"Modes" in data assimilation are singular vectors of the tangent
propagator.** They are the initial perturbations of greatest growth in a
chosen norm. `frontier.md`'s selection modes are not singular vectors of a
propagator. They are singular vectors of a sensitivity or selection matrix.
What transfers is:
- the metric lesson;
- the algorithms;
- the habit of computing modes for a stated purpose.

The dynamical reading (growth of perturbations) does not transfer [Judgement].

### 3.3 The metric question, answered by this field

Palmer et al. (1998) give the most useful single statement:
- the metric is not free;
- it is fixed by the purpose of the calculation;
- different purposes give different, equally correct, singular vectors.

For TF24 this means "the effective dimension of the field" is undefined
until the purpose is fixed. Three purposes give three metrics [Judgement]:

1. **Selection.** Use the pairing with the equilibrium field's response,
   g = −⟨S, dE*/dθ⟩. The trait × trait Jacobian of g, or the trait × species
   matrix ⟨S_i, I_j⟩, needs no field metric at all (§2.4).
2. **Response to realised variability.** Use the covariance C_E of the field
   across records or patches as the metric. Then C_E^{1/2} S is the fitness
   variation explained per unit of realistic field variation. This is
   ensemble sensitivity, and the analogue of Barkmeijer's analysis-error
   metric. Its effective rank counts the field directions that both vary and
   matter.
3. **Numerical convergence.** Use a quadrature-weighted L² metric in
   (z, ℓ, a), so the spectrum converges as the discretisation is refined.
   Unweighted per-slot coordinates make singular values depend on how many
   slots there are.

`frontier.md`'s "trait × field selection matrix" has entries
M_kf = S_f · (dE*_f/dθ_k), with no sum. It is invariant under rescaling the
units of each field coordinate, since S_f and dE*_f scale inversely. It is
not invariant under mixing coordinates (for example, light expressed in
Fourier modes over height), or under refinement unless quadrature weights
are included [Derived]. Its singular vectors therefore describe a
coordinate-wise attribution, not intrinsic field modes. H1's test should
state this, or use one of the three metrics above.

### 3.4 Transferable algorithms

- **Matrix-free randomised SVD or Lanczos** (Halko et al. 2011), with
  products by S-type matrices from adjoint passes and by I-type matrices from
  tangent passes. This is already in `frontier.md` §4.4.
- **Active subspaces for H1** (Constantine et al. 2014). Sample θ′ over the
  local-analysis range (×0.5 to ×2), compute ∇_θ′φ with one invader sweep
  each, and take the eigenvectors of the averaged outer product. That gives
  the trait directions along which fitness varies over the range, not just
  at one point. It needs no field hook, and the existing invader sweep
  supplies the gradients [Judgement]. Zahm et al. (2020) handle several
  outputs at once, such as fitness across several climates.
- **Ensemble sensitivity** (Torn & Hakim 2008) across the eight-record bank,
  as a check on adjoint field sensitivities.
- **Hessian-based uncertainty** (Kalmikov & Heimbach 2014). Low-rank
  Lanczos on Hessian-vector products gives the invader Hessian's leading
  curvature directions without forming it.

### 3.5 Where it breaks

- Data assimilation studies sensitivity to initial conditions in chaotic
  systems. TF24 studies sensitivity to traits and forcing in a dissipative
  system over a finite horizon. Adjoints are better behaved there; §4 notes
  the exception.
- Data assimilation has observations and a cost function that set the
  metric naturally. TF24 has no data term, so the purpose must be supplied.

## 4. Statistical physics linear response

### 4.1 Established results

- **Fluctuation–dissipation.** Kubo (1966): the linear response of a system
  at thermal equilibrium to a small force is given by correlation functions
  of its unforced fluctuations [Ref].
- **Climate FDT.** Leith (1975) proposed that the climate's response to
  forcing could be computed from lagged covariances of its natural
  variability, under approximate Gaussianity and stationarity [Verified via
  secondary sources]. Later work found the quasi-Gaussian form often fails
  for chaotic systems with fractal attractors [Verified, secondary source].
- **Response theory.** Ruelle (2009) reviews linear response for
  differentiable dynamical systems [Ref]. Lucarini & Sarno (2011) and
  Lucarini, Ragone & Lunkeit (2017) use it to predict climate responses to
  arbitrary forcing time patterns via Green's functions [Ref]. Gritsun &
  Branstator (2007) built an FDT response operator for a 3-D atmospheric
  model [Ref]. Hassanzadeh & Kuang (2016) built a linear response function
  from Green's functions [Ref]. Ghil & Lucarini (2020) review the field
  [Ref].
- **Chaos and adjoints.** Adjoint sensitivities of long-time averages in
  chaotic systems diverge (Lea, Allen & Haine 2000) [Ref]. Least-squares
  shadowing is one remedy (Wang, Hu & Blonigan 2014) [Ref].
- **Ecological linear response.** Press perturbations are the inverse
  community matrix (Bender, Case & Gilpin 1984) [Ref]. Small errors in that
  matrix make the predicted signs of press responses indeterminate (Yodzis
  1988) [Ref]. Novak et al. (2016) review this [Ref].

### 4.2 The correspondence

- **Susceptibility.** The static response χ = dE*/d(forcing) is TF24's
  impact I (forcing by a strategy's abundance) and dE*/dθ (forcing by
  traits). The conjugate pairing ⟨S, I⟩ is a generalised susceptibility of
  fitness [Derived].
- **The (I4) predictor** is a one-dimensional inverse Jacobian. With several
  resident species it becomes the inverse of an n × n matrix built from
  ⟨S_i, I_j⟩ and the F_{ln b} terms [Derived].
- **One fact, three readings.** The press-perturbation literature shows that
  this inverse is ill-conditioned when species are similar. That is the same
  mathematical fact as Meszéna's limiting similarity: robustness of
  coexistence and predictability of responses fail together [Judgement,
  linking established results]. It is also why the (I4) predictor degrades
  near folds.

### 4.3 What it implies

- FDT has nothing to work on in the current TF24. The regional state is
  deterministic and stationary, so there are no fluctuations [Derived].
- In a two-clock model with calendar weather, b(t) and the field fluctuate.
  Leith's estimator would then give impacts from one long run without
  perturbation experiments. The ecological system is strongly forced and
  non-Gaussian, so the estimator's known failures are expected. Adjoint and
  tangent passes, which TF24 has, are the more reliable route [Judgement].
- Green's-function climate response (Lucarini et al. 2017) is the right form
  for calendar-time forcing: the response of the regional state to any
  weather anomaly path is a convolution with a response kernel computed
  once. §6.2 and §8 develop this.
- Lea et al.'s warning applies only if the resident's regional dynamics
  under calendar forcing are chaotic. A patch's cohort dynamics over 40 years
  are dissipative and finite-horizon, so adjoints of patch-level quantities
  are well defined. Long-run regional averages with b(t) feedback should be
  checked for this [Judgement].

### 4.4 Where it breaks

- Linear response is first order. Effects of variability on mean fitness
  are second order: Jensen, relative nonlinearity, storage (`theory.md`
  B.7). Linear response gives the variance of fitness and of selection, not
  the mean effect of variance (§8.3).

## 5. Reinforcement learning and control

### 5.1 Established results

- **The policy gradient theorem.** Sutton, McAllester, Singh & Mansour
  (2000) showed that the gradient of expected return is
  Σ_s d^π(s) Σ_a ∇π(a|s) Q^π(s, a). It needs no derivative of the
  state-visitation distribution d^π [Verified: proceedings record and
  abstract summary]. Williams (1992) gave the REINFORCE estimator [Ref].
- **Natural gradients.** Amari (1998) introduced the natural gradient, the
  gradient under the Fisher-information metric [Ref]. Kakade (2001) applied
  it to policies [Verified: NIPS 14 proceedings record].
- **Reproductive value as currency.** McNamara & Houston (1986) argued that
  reproductive value is the common currency for behavioural decisions, set
  by state-dependent dynamic programming [Ref].
- **Goal-oriented error estimation.**
  - Becker & Rannacher (2001): dual-weighted residuals. The error in a goal
    functional is the residual weighted by the adjoint solution [Ref].
  - Giles & Süli (2002): adjoint error correction and extrapolation [Ref].
  - Venditti & Darmofal (2000): grid adaptation for functional outputs [Ref].
  - For ODEs, Estep (1995) and Cao & Petzold (2004): the global error in a
    functional is a sum over steps of local error times the adjoint [Ref].
  - Becker & Vexler (2005): mesh refinement aimed at the accuracy of
    parameter sensitivities, not only of the functional [Ref].
- **Multivariate selection.** Lande (1979): the response to selection is
  Δz̄ = G β, with G the additive genetic covariance matrix [Ref]. Lande &
  Arnold (1983) measure β [Ref]. Schluter (1996): adaptive divergence
  follows g_max, the "genetic line of least resistance" [Ref]. The canonical
  equation of adaptive dynamics carries a mutational covariance M (Dieckmann
  & Law 1996, in `theory.md`).

### 5.2 The correspondence

**The policy gradient theorem is the Markov-decision version of Caswell's
formula** and of `theory.md` (F.1) [Judgement, but the term-by-term mapping
is exact]:

| policy gradient | TF24 (F.1) | Caswell |
|---|---|---|
| state-visitation distribution d^π(s) | n(s, a) = p(s) σ_s ℓ_s(a), density of invaders alive | right eigenvector w |
| action value Q^π(s, a) | reproductive value and its gradient ∇_x V_s(a) | left eigenvector v |
| ∇_θ π(a \| s) | ∂G/∂θ′ | ∂A/∂θ |
| discount | survival | (none) |
| return J | R′ | λ |

In each case the derivative of the visitation distribution drops out, because
the value function (adjoint) carries it. "Value function = reproductive
value" is exact for the invader, in a held field. "Critic" = the adjoint.
"Actor" = the leaf's operating-point rule. `theory.md` F.4's result is a
statement about when a myopic actor (maximise P now) is consistent with the
critic: the control reaches the return through one currency with positive
value.

**The metric question in trait space.** The policy gradient direction
depends on the parameterisation. The natural gradient removes that
dependence by using the Fisher metric. The evolutionary counterpart is that
the direction of evolution is Gβ (or Mg), not β. This bears directly on H1
[Judgement]. Observed trait syndromes are the image of selection under G or
M, accumulated over history. A low-dimensional syndrome can come from:
- low-rank selection, which is H1;
- low-rank G, as in Schluter's lines of least resistance;
- several tasks with Pareto trade-offs: Shoval et al. (2012) showed that
  "for two tasks, phenotypes fall on the line connecting the two
  archetypes ... for three tasks, phenotypes fall within a triangle"
  [Verified, abstract];
- the trade-offs built into TF24's parameterisation (for example the lma
  hyperparameterisation), which constrain the reachable trait set before
  selection acts.

H1's test must separate these. The singular spectrum of selection should be
computed on the full, unconstrained trait vector, and separately with the
built-in trade-offs, so the two can be compared [Judgement].

**Dual-weighted residuals and node and step choice.** The objectives ask for
heuristics that choose introductions and ODE steps together so that ln J,
gradients and curvatures are within ε [Derived from Becker & Rannacher;
Estep; Cao & Petzold]:
- **ODE steps.** The error in ln J ≈ Σ over steps of (local truncation error
  vector) · (adjoint at that step). The adjoint is already on the tape.
  Weighting the step controller's local error by |λ| gives a goal-oriented
  controller: tight where reproductive value is large, loose where nothing
  downstream depends on the state.
- **Introductions.** The birth-date integral's quadrature error has a direct
  term (trapezium error in Σ p σ φ_s(T)) and, for the resident, a field
  term. The field term is the field adjoint contracted with the field error
  that a coarse cohort grid causes. The introduction hook already exposes the
  adjoint per introduction row (`prices-and-manifold.md` §1).
- **Gradients and curvatures** need error estimates for derivatives. Becker
  & Vexler (2005) show that this needs the adjoint of the sensitivity
  equation, a second-order adjoint. With XAD that is forward-over-reverse.
- **"Shared grid"** is the standard practice in adjoint-based shape and
  parameter optimisation: freeze the adapted mesh during a local analysis,
  and remesh between analyses. It is used because the computed functional is
  not smooth in parameters when the mesh changes [Judgement; common
  practice, no citation checked].
- **"Predictable"** (a looser run estimates error) is Richardson-type
  estimation. Dual-weighted residuals give a single-run estimate that can
  serve as the second opinion.

### 5.3 Where it breaks

- Reinforcement learning assumes a stationary MDP that does not respond to
  the population of policies. TF24's environment is made by the population.
  The right reinforcement-learning analogue is multi-agent learning in the
  mean-field limit, which is a mean-field game (§6.1).
- Reinforcement learning's sampling estimators (baselines, advantage
  functions) address variance in Monte Carlo gradients. TF24 has exact
  adjoints and does not need them.
- Dual-weighted residual estimates are asymptotic. They fail at regime
  switches of the leaf and at the size-asymmetry jump, where the
  linearisation they rest on is invalid.

## 6. Closer structural matches

### 6.1 Mean-field games and heterogeneous-agent macroeconomics

**Established.**
- **Mean-field games.** Lasry & Lions (2007) and Huang, Malhamé & Caines
  (2006) define mean-field games [Ref, both]. Each is a coupled system:
  - a forward Kolmogorov equation for the population density;
  - a backward Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation for the value function;
  - consistency, in that the mean field each agent responds to is generated
    by the density.

  (The Crossref record lists the 2006 authors' order as Caines, Huang,
  Malhamé; the paper itself is usually cited as Huang, Malhamé & Caines. I
  did not resolve this.)
- **Nash versus social optimum.** Carmona, Delarue & Lachapelle (2013)
  compare the mean-field game, a Nash equilibrium, with the control of
  McKean–Vlasov dynamics, a social optimum. These generally differ [Ref].
- **Heterogeneous-agent macro.** Achdou, Han, Lasry, Lions & Moll (2022)
  write continuous-time heterogeneous-agent models as mean-field games
  [Ref].
- **Idiosyncratic risk only.** Aiyagari (1994): uninsured idiosyncratic risk
  with no aggregate shocks gives a stationary distribution and constant
  aggregate prices [Ref].
- **Aggregate shocks.** Krusell & Smith (1998): when aggregate shocks make
  prices depend on the whole wealth distribution, agents can forecast prices
  almost perfectly from the mean of the distribution alone ("approximate
  aggregation") [Verified via secondary sources].
- **Linearising around the stationary state.** Reiter (2009) [Ref]. Boppart,
  Krusell & Mitman (2018) treat the impulse response to a one-time shock as
  a numerical derivative [Ref]. Auclert, Bardóczy, Rognlie & Straub (2021)
  compute "sequence-space Jacobians — the derivatives of perfect-foresight
  equilibrium mappings between aggregate sequences around the steady
  state", then compose and invert them for general-equilibrium impulse
  responses [Verified, abstract]. Their fast "fake news" algorithm uses the
  time-invariance of the steady state [Verified only via a secondary
  description].

**The correspondence [Judgement, with the mapping exact in form].**

| mean-field game / het-agent macro | TF24 |
|---|---|
| density of agents over idiosyncratic state | cohort density over (birth date, size, other states) in each patch |
| value function (backward HJB) | reproductive value (backward adjoint) |
| mean field / prices | the field E (plus b) |
| Nash mean-field equilibrium | ESS community at demographic equilibrium |
| McKean–Vlasov optimal control | the stand optimum (max b* or another group objective) |
| idiosyncratic shocks, no aggregate risk (Aiyagari) | independent weather records per patch (`theory.md` D.3 option 1) |
| aggregate shocks / common noise (Krusell–Smith) | calendar-time weather shared by all patches |
| deterministic age-earnings profile in a life-cycle model | the current model: the same record replayed along patch age |

This is the closest structural match found. It separates three climate
structures cleanly, and shows the current model is the third, the
deterministic life-cycle profile. It also answers what the aggregate state
must be in each case:
- With idiosyncratic shocks only, the aggregate is stationary and the field
  is a function of age alone.
- With common shocks, prices depend in principle on the whole distribution.
  This is the "master equation" difficulty of mean-field games with common
  noise.

**A structural advantage TF24 has over macro [Derived].** Patches interact
only through regional seed rain b(t), because dispersal is global and fields
are local to a patch. So given the path of b(t) and of the weather c(t),
every patch is an independent single-patch run. The aggregate state that
couples them is the scalar time series b(t) per species, plus p(a, t) if
disturbance responds to weather. Krusell–Smith needed an empirical finding
(approximate aggregation) to reduce the distribution to its mean. TF24's
spatial structure gives the reduction exactly.

**Where it breaks.**
- Agents in mean-field games choose controls; TF24 individuals do not,
  except the leaf's operating point. Traits change by selection. The
  mean-field-game equilibrium therefore corresponds to the ESS, not to
  ecological dynamics at fixed traits.
- Macro models have one or a few prices. TF24's "price" (the field) is
  infinite-dimensional and not common to all agents (§1.5).

### 6.2 Ecosystem demography: the two-clock domain

**Established [Verified, from the paper's text].** Moorcroft, Hurtt & Pacala
(2001) derive a size- and age-structured (SAS) first-moment approximation of
a stochastic gap model by conditioning on disturbance:
- The plant density n(z, x, a, t) depends on size z, type x, gap age a (time
  since the last disturbance) and calendar time t. It obeys

  ∂n/∂t = −∂(g_s n)/∂z_s − ∂(g_a n)/∂z_a − ∂n/∂a − μ(z, x, r̄, t) n (their Eq. 4),

  with r̄ the conditional mean resources in gaps of age a at time t.
- The gap-age distribution obeys the von Foerster equation
  ∂p/∂t = −∂p/∂a − λ(a, t) p, with ∫p da = 1 (their Eq. 5).
- Recruitment is a boundary flux at minimum size from random dispersal
  among gaps within a grid cell.
- "The precipitation rate is specified at the grid-cell level", and
  "sub-grid cell heterogeneity in water availability arises due to
  differences in evapotranspiration rates between gaps".
- Fire rates are a function of local fuel and local soil moisture, so
  disturbance depends on weather.
- The 2001 implementation used climatological forcing.
- Light within a gap decays with the leaf area above each height.

Medvigy et al. (2009) is ED2, with finer, sub-daily forcing [Ref]. Fisher
et al. (2018) review demographic vegetation models in Earth system models
[Ref]. Levin & Paine (1974) introduced the patch-age framework for disturbed
communities [Ref].

**The correspondence [Derived].** ED is exactly the two-clock domain. Patch
age a and calendar time t are separate coordinates, and climate is a
function of t shared by all gaps in a cell. TF24 is the t-stationary special
case of this PDE:
- set ∂/∂t = 0;
- take λ(a) independent of t;
- take b constant;
- index the weather by a instead of t.

**Characteristics.** Along characteristics t − a = t₀ (the disturbance
date), the PDE separates into one single-patch problem per disturbance date.
A patch disturbed at t₀ sees weather c(t₀ + a) and seed rain b(t₀ + a) at
age a. So the two-clock model is a family of TF24 patch runs indexed by t₀,
coupled by the renewal relation

 b(t) = S_D ∫ p(a, t) f(a; t − a) da,

where f(a; t₀) is the seed production per unit area of a patch of age a
disturbed at t₀. [Derived; this mirrors ED's random-dispersal boundary
condition.]

**Age, period and cohort.** In demography, age, period and cohort are
linearly dependent (cohort = period − age), so their linear effects cannot
be separated statistically (Holford 1983) [Ref]. The current TF24 aliases the
period effect (calendar weather) onto age. A mechanistic two-clock model has
no identification problem in its own derivatives: the adjoint attributes
fitness to c(t) and to a separately, because they enter differently. The
problem returns only when attributions are inferred statistically from
model output [Judgement].

### 6.3 Demographic sensitivity in random and periodic environments

- **Stochastic elasticities.** Tuljapurkar, Horvitz & Pascarella (2003)
  distinguish the stochastic growth rate, the growth rate of the average
  population and the growth rate of averaged rates. They argue "a
  fundamental tool for studying selection pressures in varying environments
  is the response of growth rate to vital rates in all habitat states"
  [Verified, abstract].
- **R0 under seasonality.** Bacaër & Ait Dads (2012) and Thieme (2009) give
  R0 in periodic and time-heterogeneous structured populations as the
  spectral radius of a next-generation operator over phase [Ref].
- **Function-valued traits.** Dieckmann, Heino & Parvinen (2006) is the
  adaptive dynamics of function-valued traits [Ref]. It is relevant if
  phenology or allocation schedules over the calendar become traits in a
  two-clock model.

## 7. The metric and basis question, gathered

Is "effective dimension of the field" basis-dependent? Yes, unless it is
defined through an invariant or a stated metric. Every field consulted agrees
on this [Judgement, summarising Palmer et al. 1998, Amari 1998 and Meszéna's
pairing]. The options:

| object | invariant under field-basis change? | invariant under refinement? | purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| g_k = −⟨S, dE*/dθ_k⟩, trait × trait Jacobian of g | yes | yes | selection; H1 |
| A_ij = ⟨S_i, I_j⟩, species × species | yes | yes | coexistence robustness, number of coexisting types; H2 |
| dφ_i/dc (through the resident, B.6), climate × strategy, whitened by climate covariance | yes (field integrated out); climate has a natural metric, its covariance | yes, if daily resolution is fixed | H2: dimensions that the climate actually varies in and that matter |
| C_E^{1/2} S, field × strategy | given C_E | given quadrature weights | response to realised field variability |
| S alone, M_kf = S_f dE*_f/dθ_k | rescaling only (M); no for S | only with quadrature weights | attribution, not dimension |

Trait-side scaling also matters. Using elasticities (log traits) fixes units.
Using G or M as the trait metric answers "which directions will evolution
move", which is a different question from "which directions does selection
point" (§5.2).

## 8. Recommendations for the domain representation

### 8.1 Two clocks: adopt ED's structure, keep the current model as the stationary base

1. **Represent the field as E(z, ℓ, a; t₀)** on characteristics, with
   t₀ = t − a. This is ED's (a, t) structure.
   - The current single-patch run is the t₀-independent case.
   - Implement two-clock runs as a family of single-patch runs over
     disturbance dates, coupled only through b(t), and through p(a, t) if
     disturbance becomes weather-dependent.
   - No new PDE solver is needed. What is new is the outer coupling and the
     per-t₀ forcing [Derived].
2. **Separate the three climate structures**, as heterogeneous-agent macro
   does:
   - (A) age-locked, the current model;
   - (B) idiosyncratic, with independent records per patch: stationary,
     deterministic regional state, `theory.md` D.3 option 1;
   - (C) common calendar weather, with aggregate shocks.

   (B) is cheapest and gives the spatial storage effect. (C) is the only
   one with regional fluctuations in b(t), temporal storage and temporal
   relative nonlinearity. Real weather is a mix of (B) and (C).
   Recommendation: build (B) first, then (C) by linearisation [Judgement].
3. **Linearise (C) in sequence space around the stationary state**, after
   Auclert et al. (2021).
   - The patch-level kernels are K_c(a, a′) = ∂f(a)/∂c(a′) and
     K_b(a, a′) = ∂f(a)/∂b(a′), both causal (a′ ≤ a).
   - The regional Jacobian is then a convolution,
     𝒥(τ) = ∫ p(a) K(a, a − τ) da, Toeplitz in calendar lag.
   - Equilibrium closure is (I − S_D 𝒥_b) δb = S_D 𝒥_c δc, solved in
     sequence space for any weather anomaly path.
   - Rows of K come from resident adjoints with the record as input (B.6).
     Columns come from tangents. K is probably low-rank, so a randomised SVD
     with a few dozen passes may suffice [Derived; the low-rank claim is a
     judgement to test].
   - Nonlinear checks are full runs for a few large droughts, as Boppart et
     al. use impulse responses.
4. **The base state for (C) must be a calendar-stationary climate**: constant
   or a mean seasonal cycle. For a seasonal cycle, invader fitness is
   Bacaër's next-generation spectral radius over disturbance phase. Weather
   anomalies are the shocks. The current age-indexed record is not that base
   state. It is a different world (A), and the bank of records measures
   sensitivity to which record is replayed, not stochastic fitness (as
   `theory.md` D.2 says) [Judgement].
5. **Weather-dependent disturbance is a separate decision.** ED ties fire
   to soil moisture and fuel. If disturbance depends on weather, patch age
   and weather are correlated for a physical reason, and p(a, t) becomes an
   aggregate state. Model (A) is then a crude form of a real correlation
   [Judgement].

### 8.2 Are field sensitivities the right object?

**Yes, as the first-order dual object for the invader. Not alone, for
dimension claims.**

- **What S is.** S is exact. It is the structured-population sensitivity
  niche vector of Szilágyi & Meszéna (2009), and it is the shadow price that
  every adjacent field identifies with the costate or Lagrange multiplier.
  Keep it, and cite that paper.
- **For H1 and H2, contract it.** Contract S with something that lives in
  the same space: impacts I, the equilibrium response dE*/dθ, or a stated
  covariance. Or move the analysis to climate space through the two-stage
  adjoint (dφ/dc), where the climate's own covariance supplies a natural
  metric. That avoids choosing a field metric at all. For H2 in particular,
  measure effective rank in climate space: whitened climate-sensitivity
  kernels across strategies, on records of equal mean and different
  variability [Judgement].
- **In a two-clock model, S gains the index t₀**, and its size multiplies by
  the number of disturbance dates resolved. That is the Arrow–Debreu count.
  The coupling remains the scalar b(t), so the cost of the regional problem
  does not grow with the field's size [Derived].
- **S is first order.** The mean effect of climate variability on fitness is
  second order: E[δφ] ≈ ½ tr(H_EE C_E) + cross terms. Here H_EE is the
  invader's Hessian in field space and C_E the field covariance that the
  sequence-space Jacobian delivers (C_E = 𝒥 C_c 𝒥ᵀ).
  - The trace can be estimated without forming H_EE. Use Hutchinson's (1989)
    estimator [Ref]: average zᵀ H_EE z over random z drawn with covariance
    C_E. Each sample costs one Hessian-vector product (forward-over-reverse).
  - This is Chesson's small-variance expansion carried out in field space
    [Derived]. It is what tests the non-equilibrium half of H2 (§2.3).
  - First-order linearisation alone implies certainty equivalence: no effect
    of variance on means. That is a known limitation of linearised macro
    solutions and applies here equally [Judgement].

### 8.3 Concrete next steps, in order of cost

1. **Active subspace of invader fitness over the local-analysis trait
   range.** Needs only existing invader sweeps. A first test of H1 that
   needs no field hook.
2. **The trait × trait selection Jacobian and its spectrum**, via chords, on
   the six smooth climates. Basis-free in the field.
3. **Field hook plus the two-stage adjoint**, to get dφ_i/dc for about 20
   strategies on records of equal mean. Whitened rank in climate space gives
   H2's first part.
4. **Check the effective-rank prediction from soil memory** (§2.2): rank
   ≲ record length / memory time × layers.
5. **Idiosyncratic-records model (B).** M resident runs at a common b.
6. **Sequence-space linearisation of the common-weather model (C)**, then
   Hutchinson traces for the second-order mean effect of variability.
7. **Goal-oriented step control** weighted by |λ|, with second-order
   adjoints for derivative targets. Do this alongside the scheduling work,
   not after it.

## 9. Where each analogy breaks: summary

| field | strongest correspondence | breaks because |
|---|---|---|
| general equilibrium | free entry ↔ ESS; LP duality ↔ competitive exclusion; Pigouvian price ↔ λ^res − λ^inv; correspondence principle ↔ sign of (I4) | no exchange, so no common price; field is quantity, prices are strategy-specific; no normative optimum |
| niche theory | S and I are Szilágyi–Meszéna's sensitivity and impact for structured populations; ⟨S_i, I_j⟩ is basis-free | light is pre-empted, not pooled; size asymmetry makes fitness non-smooth; Levin needs linearity |
| data assimilation | B.6 is 4D-Var's forcing gradient; Kauker-style event attribution; metric fixed by purpose | DA modes are propagator singular vectors (growth); ours are sensitivity spectra; no data term to fix the metric |
| linear response | impact = susceptibility; ill-conditioning ↔ limiting similarity; Green's functions for calendar forcing | no fluctuations in the current model; FDT's quasi-Gaussian form unreliable; first order only |
| RL and control | policy gradient theorem = Caswell = (F.1); natural gradient ↔ G/M metric; dual-weighted residuals for steps and nodes | environment is made by the population (needs mean-field games); dual-weighted residuals fail at jumps |
| mean-field games / het-agent macro | forward density + backward value + consistency; Nash vs McKean–Vlasov = ESS vs stand optimum; idiosyncratic vs aggregate shocks = climate structures (B) vs (C); sequence-space Jacobians | traits are not controls; the field is not a common price |
| ED | exact two-clock PDE in (size, a, t) with shared calendar climate; TF24 is its stationary case | ED is a first-moment closure built for prediction, not for evolution or differentiation |
