All three have live pathways, and they share one structural unlock the record has never used.

**1. Zero-weight ghost members: the mean-field structure makes the lineage axis cheaply explorable, and nobody has exploited it.** Members interact *only* through the aggregates `a` and `s`. A member carried with zero contribution to both aggregates — its own state, own `ρ`-law, own inner argmax, excluded from the sums — is therefore an *exact* passive probe of "what would a member inserted at `τ_ins` do," up to the self-feedback it would have exerted, which is O(its mass fraction). Given one saved forward solution's `(u, s)(t)` fields, integrating a ghost costs ~1/M of a solve, is embarrassingly parallel, and can take its *own* tiny steps through its own C⁰ events without touching anything global. And the approximation is best exactly where you need it most: the 1532× spike in §2c is by definition a *marginal* member (`ρ → 0` on one branch), so its neglected feedback vanishes at the crossing. Validation is one experiment: insert the same candidate as ghost and as real member, compare `g(τ_ins)` and the crossing location — that measures the frozen-field error directly and gates everything below.

**2. Replace the refiner with goal-oriented placement from one solve, and get a J error bar for free.** The refiner is expensive because it discovers insertion value by re-running full forwards. But `g(τ_ins)` — the per-slot J-contribution you already computed in §2c — supports a hierarchical-surplus estimator from a *single* solve: the interpolation defect of `g_j` against its lineage-neighbours, already in J-units, densified wherever it's ambiguous by a ghost sweep of the axis. Equidistribute that surplus (it automatically concentrates at small `τ_ins`, since `w(τ_ins)` is inside `g`) and treat spike slots separately; then one real verification solve closes the loop, and the number of outer iterations needed *is* your measurement of the self-consistency feedback — which is the open question your 6× datum poses (mis-placed quadrature mass, or field shift from the added mass? **5. The architectural stone: windowed waveform relaxation — iterate to self-consistency instead of freezing, which is the one decoupling §6c never tried.** Every recorded decomposition failure is a zeroth-order hold (held-`a` deletes `∂a/∂u`, plateaus at 0.1) or re-pays O(M) inside a fast sub-cycle (6–25×). Picard/waveform iteration does neither: hold the *function* `a(t)` from the previous sweep, integrate the 5-dimensional `u`-chain against it (trivial), integrate all M members against the resulting `(u,s)(t)` — each member *independently, in parallel, with its own adaptive steps* — reassemble `a(t)`, repeat. The fixed point is the exact coupled solution, so the plateau mechanism is gone by construction. What it buys is exactly what 2b diagnosed as the pathology: no global max-norm, so no collective punishment where one component of 800 rejects an O(M) step; rejections cost one member's work, not M; members stop being forced to the fast block's stepping; the whole sweep is GPU-shaped. The risk is loop gain — your held-`a` plateau says the `x↔u` feedback is first-order, so plain Picard may be marginal — but the iterate lives in a tiny space (`a(t)` is 5 functions plus scalar `s(t)`), which is ideal for Anderson acceleration, and windowing bounds the gain. The kill-or-fund test costs one member sweep on saved fields: perturb `a(t)` by δ, run one sweep, measure the contraction factor per window length. Two honest flags: the gradient seam changes (differentiate the converged fixed point via IFT — one adjoint sweep, standard, and the FD reference re-solves the relaxation as run), and dense `(u,s)(t)` output must be stored (5+1 dimensions; cheap, but splining it introduces knots you now know how to audit).
